# Cerebras Erases I/O; M4 Caps Near 1.25×

Cerebras inference does not split I/O time — it deletes the per-token weight read by keeping the entire served model in on-chip SRAM beside the compute, and that single physical fact is why it runs at hundreds to thousands of tokens/s while a 16 GB M4 stalls at 2–7. On the M4 the honest answer is **no**: no software method reaches bit-exact Qwen 5+ or Nemotron 15+ tok/s on this hardware, because every scheme that merely retimes, reorders, prefetches, or overlaps *the same bytes* is capped near **1.25×** — the measured gain of a perfect one-token-lookahead oracle that left physical bytes essentially unchanged (2.19→2.72 tok/s, −1.8% bytes; [E1 result](../docs/performance/E1_ORACLE_RESULT.md)). Beating that cap requires either fewer bytes per token or more fast bandwidth, and on this machine both doors are effectively shut: the models move 200–870 MB/token across a single SSD that saturates at ~3.36 GB/s, and the 16 GB pool auto-sizes to zero under resident weights, KV, and OS. Cerebras's speed is a capacity-and-bandwidth artifact of a wafer, not an algorithm a laptop can emulate; the transferable lesson is only the limiting principle that every off-chip weight read is a tax to minimize, amortize, and overlap. A handful of cheap bit-exact experiments remain worth running, but they are partial gains, not routes to the targets.

## Cerebras Keeps Every Served Weight in SRAM Next to the Datapath

The physical substrate is the difference, not a software trick: WSE-3 is one 300 mm wafer (46,225 mm², ~57× a large reticle-sized die) carrying **~900,000 programmable cores and 44 GB of distributed SRAM**, with roughly **50% of the silicon given over to SRAM** and **~21 PB/s aggregate on-chip memory bandwidth** ([Cerebras press release](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-announces-third-generation-wafer-scale-engine); [SemiAnalysis, "Faster Tokens Please"](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/cerebras-faster-tokens-please); [arXiv:2503.11698](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11698v1)). Each core has ~48 kB of local SRAM addressed independently — there is "no shared memory in the traditional sense," all sharing is explicit over the fabric — so a weight operand is read tens of microns from the FMAC datapath at full datapath bandwidth ([Architecture Deep Dive](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning)). WSE-3 Turbo in CS-4 raises this to **43.2 PB/s** with a **~2 µs wafer-to-wafer hop** ([CS-4](https://www.cerebras.ai/cs4)). The consequence is the property that matters here: **the inference path performs zero off-chip weight reads per token**. The arithmetic behind it is blunt — a 70B model at 16-bit is 140 GB, so 10 tok/s needs ~1.4 TB/s and ~1,000 tok/s needs ~140 TB/s, "far exceeding the memory bandwidth of any GPU server"; Cerebras removes the transfer by storing the weights on-chip ([Introducing Cerebras Inference](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/introducing-cerebras-inference-ai-at-instant-speed)). A 20B model fits one wafer (44 GB ≈ 40 GB at 16-bit); 70B spans as few as four wafers, split at layer boundaries.

Crucially, this residency claim applies to **inference**, and must not be conflated with Cerebras's **training** model. In training and trillion-parameter scale, weights live in external **MemoryX** DRAM/flash and are *streamed* onto the wafer one layer at a time: each weight triggers an AXPY on a batch of resident activations and is then discarded, gradients stream back to MemoryX, and weights are "never stored on the CS-3 system, not even temporarily" ([Architecture Deep Dive](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning); [Weight Streaming](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/linear-scaling-made-possible-with-weight-streaming)). That is a throughput-over-batch optimization for weights too large to hold. The served 8B/70B/120B inference models take the opposite path — the whole model is resident, so there is no per-token weight stream at all ([Introducing Cerebras Inference](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/introducing-cerebras-inference-ai-at-instant-speed)). Confusing the two would miss the point of this research: the near-zero I/O wait of *served* inference comes from residency, not from streaming cleverness.

The published speed trajectory shows that this hardware advantage only became a product advantage after a software rewrite. Launch in August 2024 delivered 1,800 tok/s on Llama-3.1-8B and 450 tok/s on 70B; by October 2024, rewritten MatMul/reduce/activation kernels, **asynchronous wafer I/O overlapped with compute**, and **speculative decoding** (small draft model plus large target in tandem) lifted 70B to 2,100 tok/s with unchanged 16-bit precision ([3× faster](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-inference-3x-faster)); DeepSeek-R1-Distill-70B reached >1,500 tok/s ([press release](https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-launches-worlds-fastest-deepseek-r1-distill-llama-70b-inference)); and GPT-OSS-120B MoE reached **>3,000 tok/s**, versus 650 tok/s for Baseten's 8×GB200 Blackwell stack with TP8, TensorRT-LLM, Dynamo, and EAGLE-3 ([Blackwell vs Cerebras](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/blackwell-vs-cerebras)). By 2026 the shared catalog lists `gpt-oss-120b` at ~3,000 tok/s and `qwen-3.8-27b` at ~1,850 tok/s ([inference docs](https://inference-docs.cerebras.ai/models/overview)). Note the honest nuance in current docs: "selective weight-only quantization *only during storage*" with on-the-fly dequantization to high-precision math — activations, attention, and KV cache stay full precision — so 2024's "native 16-bit only" is no longer literally true, though storage quantization breaks bit-exactness relative to the original weights ([inference docs](https://inference-docs.cerebras.ai/models/overview)).

## Dataflow and Layer Pipelining Turn Residency Into Throughput

Residency alone would be useless if every operand still had to be fetched through a central bus, and Cerebras removes that fetch too. Each core is a dataflow engine: "all the computation is triggered by the data," with the fabric carrying both data and its associative control tag, and ML instructions loaded statically upfront so they never change during execution ([Architecture Deep Dive](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning); [WSE whitepaper](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bojieli/ai-infra-book/main/references/text/cerebras-wse3.txt)). A transformer layer is mapped onto the 2-D grid with hidden dimension H across x and batch×sequence across y; activations sit in the SRAM of the cores that will use them, weights and commands are broadcast down columns so each arriving weight fires an FMAC/AXPY into a local accumulator, and partial sums are ring-reduced along a row using static fabric "colors," with **8 microthreads** overlapping each reduction with the next weight row's FMAC. The 2-D mesh uses 5-port routers with single-cycle node-to-node latency and native broadcast/multicast, and whole MatMuls up to 100,000×100,000 need no partitioning — the entire wafer works on one layer at a time. This is why the von Neumann bottleneck disappears: program and operands are both resident and local, coordination is data-driven over a low-latency mesh, and compute moves to the weights rather than weights to a distant ALU.

For models that exceed one wafer, Cerebras uses **layer-boundary pipeline parallelism**, not tensor or expert parallelism: each wafer holds entire layers (a dense layer plus KV, or two linear-attention layers) in its SRAM and the weights never move; **only activations cross wafer-to-wafer**, by RDMA over Ethernet at roughly **2 µs per hop**, and tokens stream through the pipeline with high throughput coming from many requests staggered across stages ([direct Cerebras notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/direct_cerebras_notes.md); [CS-4](https://www.cerebras.ai/cs4); [SemiAnalysis CS-4](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/cerebrass-next-generation-cs-4-fast)). SemiAnalysis notes the constraint that makes this design inevitable: off-wafer I/O is so limited that sharding strategies needing high-bandwidth collectives at each layer are "categorically ruled out," and pipeline parallelism is the only real option because activations are small relative to weights ([SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/cerebras-faster-tokens-please)). CS-4 extends the idea by **disaggregating prefill from decode** — cheap GPUs handle prefill, the wafer specializes in decode — and claims >1,000 tok/s even on models exceeding tens of trillions of parameters ([CS-4](https://www.cerebras.ai/cs4)). MoE fits naturally: because one wafer has ~900× a single GPU's on-chip SRAM, big experts reside on-chip with no all-to-all token shuffling (the GPU expert-parallel path spends ~91% of an MoE layer in communication), and Cerebras's published fix for sparse-expert underutilization is **Batch Tiling on Attention (BTA)** — a *training* optimization that decouples attention's small-batch memory need from experts' large-batch utilization need on Qwen3-30B-A3B ([MoE at Scale](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/moe-guide-scale)). BTA is not the reason GPT-OSS decode is fast; that remains on-chip residency. Speculative decoding is used across these serves as the "fewer bytes" lever — Cerebras uses a draft model too — which is worth remembering when comparing its headline tok/s to a batch-1 local decode ([direct Cerebras notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/direct_cerebras_notes.md)).

## Three Exits From the Memory Wall — and Cerebras Takes "Change the Memory"

The I/O wall has only three principled exits, a framework recorded in the research notes and consistent with the SRAM-versus-HBM analysis in the trade press ([direct Cerebras notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/direct_cerebras_notes.md); [SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/cerebras-faster-tokens-please)): read **fewer bytes** per token, use **more parts** in parallel, or **change the memory** to one that is faster and closer to compute. Cerebras is the purest case of the third — move weights out of DRAM/HBM into SRAM that is 10–100× faster per byte and sit it directly beside the datapath — and its entire 44 GB/wafer design exists to buy that exit at the cost of capacity ([direct Cerebras notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/direct_cerebras_notes.md)). Groq's LPU is the same idea with a small SRAM and a fully deterministic compiled schedule (~230–500 MB of working SRAM per chip, ~80 TB/s), while a GPU accepts off-chip reads and maximizes HBM bandwidth (~3 TB/s on H100, ~8 TB/s on B200) with batching and reuse ([Groq LPU](https://groq.com/blog/the-groq-lpu-explained); [NVIDIA Groq 3 LPX](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-groq-3-lpx-the-low-latency-inference-accelerator-for-the-nvidia-vera-rubin-platform/); [arXiv:2503.11698](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11698v1)).

| Exit from the wall | Mechanism | Who uses it | Open on a 16 GB M4? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fewer bytes/token** | Quantization, MoE active slice, speculative decoding (one read emits several tokens) | Everyone; Cerebras's numbers involve a draft model and 4-bit experts | **Mostly closed** — Q4 affine weights already; further reduction is lossy |
| **More parts** | Tensor/expert parallelism sums bandwidth, but each layer ends in a cross-chip sync | GPU clusters, multi-Mac setups | **Closed** — no second SSD/Mac; sync costs more than bytes saved past a few parts |
| **Change the memory** | Move weights DRAM/HBM → SRAM beside compute | Cerebras (44 GB/wafer), Groq (~0.2–8 GB) | **Closed** — no wafer SRAM; 16 GB unified memory shared with KV and OS |

The table is the structural answer to the user's question. On the M4, "change the memory" is hardware-excluded, "more parts" is hardware-excluded, and "fewer bytes" is closed by the bit-exactness gate — which leaves only retiming and overlap, and those cannot change the number of bytes or the bandwidth. That is why the ceiling is so low.

## On This M4, Retiming the Same Bytes Caps at 1.25×

Two local experiments settle what "splitting I/O time" can actually do. **E0** proved the overlap premise: with 8 reader threads pulling 4 MiB `F_NOCACHE` buffers over a 113 GB checkpoint, physical SSD read bandwidth was **3.36 GB/s** while a decode step inflated only 130.1→143.2 ms (**+10.08%**, far under the 25% kill line), meaning GPU compute and SSD reads genuinely overlap and the drive is not serialized with compute ([E0 result](../docs/performance/E0_CONCURRENCY_RESULT.md)). **E1** then established the ceiling: a *perfect* one-token-lookahead oracle replaying the real greedy route raised decode from 2.19 to **2.72 tok/s (+24.6%)**, cut per-token time by 90 ms, but changed physical bytes by only **−1.8%** (873.1→857.5 MB), so the entire win was overlap, not fewer bytes ([E1 result](../docs/performance/E1_ORACLE_RESULT.md)). Its own arithmetic is decisive: ~873 MB/token ÷ 3.36 GB/s ≈ **260 ms of drive time** against ~200 ms of compute, giving an absolute ceiling near **3.8 tok/s at that byte volume — and that requires a perfect predictor**. Real zero-training predictors reach only ~0.36 recall ([E1 result](../docs/performance/E1_ORACLE_RESULT.md); [E3 predictor recall](../docs/performance/E3_PREDICTOR_RECALL.md)), so even 2.72 is an upper bound no implementation can reach, and 5/15 are far beyond it. An independent Amdahl framing gives the same answer from the other side: with ~75% of the budget in SSD wait, even magically eliminating all GPU and router work caps the gain at ~1.33× ([Apple Neural Engine integration report](../reports/Apple%20Neural%20Engine%20integration.md)).

The long-trace analysis converts this into hard, model-specific ceilings. For the steady state, first-touch (compulsory) misses fall to ~1.4% by 1,200 tokens, but capacity misses *grow* with length (Nemotron 0.060@200 → 0.110@1200), so the steady state is a real, lower plateau, not 100% hit ([STEP_B long trace](../docs/performance/STEP_B_LONG_TRACE_FINAL.md)). Reaching **Nemotron 15 tok/s needs ≥~10 GB of unique expert RAM *and* a near-Belady policy** (OPT@10 GB = 15.22 tok/s; the deployable S3-FIFO policy gets only 12.38 there), while **Qwen 5 tok/s needs ≥~12 GB plus near-OPT** (OPT@12 GB = 5.22; S3-FIFO = 3.92). Production on 16 GB auto-sizes the pool to zero, and an 8 GB pool already caused regressions and memory pressure, so neither working set is realizable ([STEP_B long trace](../docs/performance/STEP_B_LONG_TRACE_FINAL.md)). One correction matters and should be carried forward: Nemotron decode in production reads **~200 MB/token**, not the 857 MB of a no-pool profile arm, because the OS page cache is already at ~74% hit ([OPUS_SECOND_VALIDATION](../docs/performance/OPUS_SECOND_VALIDATION.md)); this makes the squared-ReLU byte-saving proposition look better on paper, but its actual test failed — the zeros are real (94% of relu2 activations exactly zero) yet scattered so that **82.5% of 64-neuron quantization groups still need reading**, yielding only ~9% total expert saving and missing the kill criterion ([RELU2 Phase 0](../docs/performance/RELU2_PHASE0_RESULT.md)).

Time-slicing is void by arithmetic rather than by measurement: with ~260 ms of drive work in a 200 ms period (5 tok/s, ρ ≈ 1.3) or a 67 ms period (15 tok/s, ρ ≈ 3.9), the device is oversubscribed and no deadline scheduler can keep the decoder from waiting; macOS exposes no storage scheduler anyway, only `MTLIOPriority.high` ([I/O splitting methods](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/io_splitting_methods.md)). This is why "split I/O time" on an oversubscribed single device is largely an accounting illusion — the bytes still cross one 3.36 GB/s link.

## Split-I/O Systems Either Overlap or Need Hardware — a Verdict by Method

The recent systems literature confirms the E0/E1 structure: overlap and prefetch hide *latency* but cannot remove *bytes*, and the big speedups come from hardware the M4 does not have. CoPilotIO (OSDI'26) is the most literal "split": GPUs initiate I/O, **CPU cores act as I/O co-pilots polling for completion**, with split SQ/CQ, hardware barriers, a lock-free barrier table, and adaptive CPU-GPU co-polling; it cuts I/O-induced stalls up to 55.5% and gives MoE 1.44× by letting I/O warps yield instead of occupying SMs during NVMe polling ([CoPilotIO, OSDI'26](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-chen-guanyi.pdf)). On the M4 the *diagnosis* is interesting — moving the wait off the critical path to a dedicated CPU thread with event signaling was never tried — but E0 already shows the GPU/SSD overlap with only ~10% inflation, so the stall available to remove is small, and Python's GIL and unified memory differ from CUDA SM polling ([direct I/O split notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/direct_io_split_notes.md)). Weave (arXiv:2609.21483) and COMET (arXiv:2609.07536) dynamically partition SMs into communication and compute workers per layer with a cost model inside a persistent megakernel, reporting 2.89× MoE-layer and 1.33× end-to-end on 4×H100 ([Weave](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21483); [COMET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07536)). There are no SMs to split on an M4, and the plausible substitutes (CPU SME, ANE) share the same unified-memory bus, so they add no SSD bandwidth. CoX-MoE, TriMoE, and DAOP use CPU matrix units for some experts (1.7–8.2× on server hardware), but on the M4 SME/AMX shares the bus and bf16 bit-exactness against MLX's Metal GEMM is unproven; the closest Apple measurement shows only ~12% on a compute-bound MLP with explicit contention warnings ([CoX-MoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17889); [TriMoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01058); [DAOP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10375); [Diffio, Apple Silicon](https://diffio.ai/blog/diffio-apple-silicon)).

| Method | What it does | Verdict on this M4 (bit-exact, one 3.36 GB/s SSD) |
|---|---|---|
| **CoPilotIO** (OSDI'26) | Offload NVMe polling from GPU warps to CPU co-pilots | Bit-exact scheduling; E0 already overlaps at +10%, so small headroom; split-poll idea untried but low payoff ([source](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-chen-guanyi.pdf)) |
| **Weave / COMET** | Dynamic per-layer SM partition into comm vs compute | Not transferable — no SMs to split; SME/ANE share the bus ([Weave](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21483), [COMET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07536)) |
| **CoX-MoE / TriMoE / DAOP** | CPU matrix units compute some experts | ~0 I/O gain; shared bus, bf16 bit-identity unproven ([CoX-MoE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17889)) |
| **Double buffering / ping-pong** | Load layer L+1 while computing L | Textbook but bounded by E1's +25%; plain form already rejected; single-sync slot-remap variant is Linux/CUDA with a retracted headline ([PR #31469](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/31469); [E1](../docs/performance/E1_ORACLE_RESULT.md)) |
| **Async I/O** (io_uring, libaio, GCD, kqueue, GDS) | Deep queues, nonblocking submission, storage DMA | Linux/NVIDIA-only or no new capability; macOS regular-file kqueue returns EPERM without a thread pool; no Apple GDS ([io_uring(7)](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/io_uring.7.html); [a10](https://github.com/Thomasdezeeuw/a10)) |
| **MTLIO** (`MTLIOCommandQueue`, `loadBytes`) | Async file→MTLBuffer on a separate queue, event-synced with compute | Feasible and bit-exact, but only **warm** reads measured (13.1–13.3 GB/s) and only 5.4–7.5% of misses were warm; **cold untested** ([Apple resource loading](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/resource-loading); [WWDC22](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10104)) |
| **`F_RDADVISE` / `F_RDADVISEV`** | Nonblocking kernel read-ahead, up to 64 ranges per syscall | Strongest untested I/O lever: 24 MB issued in ~0.5 ms, project probe **3.61–3.78 GB/s** cold-ish; must be advice-only (speculative *use* diverged tokens). Bounded by +25% ([fcntl(2)](https://keith.github.io/xcode-man-pages/fcntl.2.html); [Bun](https://github.com/oven-sh/bun/commit/adc354d9983fe1866ceeb28e6a6ecd30ce443cca); [apple_io.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/apple_io.md)) |
| **Time-slicing / deadline I/O scheduling** | Reserve/prioritize SSD slots so decoder never waits | Impossible in principle here — ρ ≈ 1.3–3.9; only `MTLIOPriority.high` is exposed ([I/O splitting methods](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/io_splitting_methods.md)) |
| **`simdgroup_async_copy` / zero-copy import** | Device→threadgroup DMA; wrap host pointer as MTLBuffer | Red herrings: async copy starts *after* bytes reach unified memory, is closed on Metal 4 and slower than manual loads; zero-copy just defers page-map cost ([bearblog RE](https://ighoshsubho.bearblog.dev/reverse-engineering-apples-simdgroup-async-copy-on-m4); [OptMLX](https://atomgradient.github.io/OptMLX)) |

The pattern across every row is the one the research notes converge on: methods in the top half overlap or repartition existing bytes and are capped by E1; methods that could add bandwidth or remove bytes need a second device, a near-data controller, or wafer SRAM ([I/O splitting methods](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/io_splitting_methods.md); [tiering/cache notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/tiering_cache.md)). None creates new SSD bandwidth on a single 16 GB M4.

## Source Quality and Gaps

These findings rest on very different evidence grades, and the distinction matters. **Vendor primary sources** (Cerebras blogs, press releases, CS-4 page, Apple developer docs) are authoritative on *specifications and API semantics* — 44 GB SRAM, 21/43.2 PB/s, 900k cores, the 2 µs hop, `F_RDADVISE` semantics, MTLIO's queue model — but every Cerebras tok/s comparison is first-party and should be read as a claim, not an independent benchmark; even the third-party comparison paper arXiv:2503.11698 is internally inconsistent about on-chip memory (44 GB / 0.05 GB / 0.4 GB in different tables) and should be used for direction, not exact figures ([arXiv:2503.11698](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11698v1); [cerebras.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/cerebras.md)). **Peer-reviewed and conference sources** — CoPilotIO at OSDI'26, ZipMoE at ICML 2026, MoE-Lightning at ASPLOS'25, SolidAttention at FAST'26 — give mechanism evidence, but most speedups are against custom or weak baselines on Jetson, T4, H100, or server setups, not on a 16 GB M4 ([CoPilotIO](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi26-chen-guanyi.pdf); [ZipMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21198); [MoE-Lightning](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11217); [SolidAttention](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast26-zheng.pdf)). Many 2026 items are single-team preprints (Weave, COMET, SP-MoE) with unreplicated ratios. **Hobbyist reverse engineering** is credible where it is reproducible but toolchain-specific: the M4 `simdgroup_async_copy` result is a single author on macOS 26/Xcode 26.4 ([bearblog RE](https://ighoshsubho.bearblog.dev/reverse-engineering-apples-simdgroup-async-copy-on-m4)), the NAND/MSP topology observation is one `ioreg` command on an M3 Pro ([zenn](https://zenn.dev/ext_ta1/articles/apple_nand_architecture?locale=en)), and MLX zero-copy findings are from community probes ([metal-linalg](https://c0rmac.github.io/metal-linalg/docs/proposals/known-buffers.md)).

The gaps are substantive and should temper any firm conclusion. There is no public teardown of the exact SRAM mapping for *inference* 70B/120B serving (whether weights ever spill under specific batch/concurrency conditions), and no published absolute TTFT millisecond values or prefill throughput for recent models ([cerebras.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/cerebras.md)). The assignment's "memory prefix" did not resolve to any named Cerebras feature and most likely refers to KV-cache/prefix residency, not a distinct technology ([cerebras.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/cerebras.md)). On the Apple side there is **no end-to-end bit-exact M4/16 GB measurement of any candidate** for these exact models, no cold-MTLIO or cold `F_RDADVISEV` result, the cause of an earlier `F_RDADVISE` EPERM is unknown (it is not a documented RDADVISE error, so likely a struct/fd/sandbox mistake), and all GPU/SME microbenchmarks found were on M4 Pro/Max or M5, so their numbers are upper bounds for a fanless base M4 Air ([apple_io.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/apple_io.md); [I/O splitting methods](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/io_splitting_methods.md)).

## The Bottom Line and the Few Experiments Still Worth Running

A 16 GB M4 **cannot** reach bit-exact Qwen 5+ or Nemotron 15+ tok/s with no quality loss by any software method. The targets are memory-and-bandwidth thresholds: more fast memory (wafer SRAM; 24–32 GB+ unified memory, where fully resident Nemotron is ~13.9 tok/s at ~20.8 GiB and 64 GB+ reaches 12–16), a second SSD (~5.5–6.5 GB/s aggregate — though a TB4 external drive is bus-limited to ~1.5 GB/s), or a second Mac (2× M4, ~25 tok/s — [Faster Qwen Nemotron report](../reports/Faster%20Qwen%20Nemotron%20inference%20on%20M4.md); [E1 result](../docs/performance/E1_ORACLE_RESULT.md); [STEP_B](../docs/performance/STEP_B_LONG_TRACE_FINAL.md)). All are excluded by the "no new hardware" rule, so the honest disposition is to report 5/15 as unreachable on this configuration rather than force a positive result.

Four cheap, bit-exact experiments are still worth doing, and none is predicted to reach the targets: **sole-pool + `F_NOCACHE` A/B** — the one untested combination, whose ceiling is page-cache parity minus an ~8% read penalty (predicted neutral-to-regression; Qwen toward ~3.2, not 5); **SHA-256 cross-layer expert dedup audit** — an offline hash pass with a huge 2× upside if duplicates exist but expected ~0 duplicates in a converged checkpoint; **advice-only `F_RDADVISE` end-to-end decode** — the only measured >wall cold-ish path, bounded by the +25% perfect-prefetch ceiling and gated on bit-identical token IDs; and **scale/bias exponent coding** — a genuine ~6% Qwen / ~3% Nemotron byte saving that is free but far too small to move the 10–12 GB working-set gap ([tiering/cache notes](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/tiering_cache.md); [apple_io.md](../research_notes/Cerebras%20inference%20and%20IO%20splitting/apple_io.md); [OPUS_SECOND_MIRACLE_RESPONSE](../docs/performance/OPUS_SECOND_MIRACLE_RESPONSE.md)). The squared-ReLU byte lever, once the most promising new idea, has already been tested and killed by Phase 0, and read-once dedup, per-layer certificates, and ANE are all DROP ([RELU2 Phase 0](../docs/performance/RELU2_PHASE0_RESULT.md); [OPUS_SECOND_VALIDATION](../docs/performance/OPUS_SECOND_VALIDATION.md)).

## Conclusion

The deeper implication is that "splitting I/O time" is mostly an accounting illusion on an oversubscribed single device: the bytes still cross one 3.36 GB/s link, so retiming them flattens the schedule but cannot shorten it, and the only real exits are fewer bytes or more bandwidth — both closed under the gate. Cerebras's lesson is therefore partly negative for the laptop: it demonstrates that the memory wall is a *capacity* problem solved by putting memory next to compute, which is exactly the one thing a fixed 16 GB M4 cannot be asked to do in software. The practical consequence for Veystrix is to stop spending experiments on overlap and prefetch variants already bounded by E1, and to spend them on the four cheap bit-exact A/Bs — while treating 5/15 tok/s as a hardware threshold to report honestly, not a target to engineer around.
